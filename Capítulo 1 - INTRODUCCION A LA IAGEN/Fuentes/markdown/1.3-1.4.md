## **Abstract** 

Software quality is undergoing a profound transformation, driven by state-of-the-art research on the application of emerging technologies in software development processes. Specifically, the use of generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) may represent an opportunity to advance the state of practice in this domain. This study aims to assess the industrial readiness and availability of Generative AI-based solutions for software quality, classifying them according to ISO/IEC 25010 attributes and SDLC phases. An empirical assessment of the state of practice was conducted, employing a Rapid Multivocal Literature Review (RMLR) protocol as a data collection instrument to screen evidence from academic databases (Scopus) and grey literature (Google, GitHub, PapersWithCode). We identified 24 potentially usable solutions. However, the analysis reveals a low technological maturity, with most solutions being academic prototypes hampered by fundamental technical limitations and adoption challenges. These include the “last mile problem” in translating research prototypes into reliable, production-ready tools; the “strategic adoption dilemma” forcing practitioners to trade off between proprietary lock-in and high open-source infrastructure costs; and the “scarcity of realistic public data,” which drives a generalization gap due to reliance on synthetic or leaked benchmarks. Generative AI in software quality remains an emerging but immature field, hampered by a critical reliability gap between academic prototypes and industrial needs. Advancing this domain requires moving beyond a narrow code-centric focus to address the quality of the AI systems themselves, expanding research across all SDLC phases and ISO 25010 attributes. We conclude with a roadmap advocating for contamination-free benchmarks, explainable architectures, and robust guidelines for real-world integration.

## **1 Introduction** 

Software Engineering (SE) has undergone profound transformations in recent decades, driven by emerging technologies, with a recent emphasis on Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI), whose potential to impact software quality processes significantly has been widely researched. Recent studies have described expectations of significant productivity gains across various stages of the Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC), driven by these new technologies. Specifically in software quality, there is growing adoption of Generative AI-based solutions for SE activities such as vulnerability detection (Kalouptsoglou et al., 2025, 2026), test generation and automation (Alagarsamy et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2024), defect localization (Qin et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026), code verification and security (Fakhoury et al., 2024; Washizaki, 2024). These solutions have been recognized for their ability to reduce human intervention, accelerate project schedules, decrease manual effort, shorten development time, and consequently contribute to improving software quality (Banh et al., 2025; Hou et al., 2024; Raghi et al., 2024). 

However, software quality is a multifaceted concept. To assess the potential effectiveness of GenAI in these solutions, it is important to define quality beyond subjective metrics and align it with standardized models that subdivide quality into specific characteristics, such as functional suitability, reliability, performance efficiency, security, and maintainability (ISO/IEC, 2011). This model type provides researchers and practitioners with a systematic approach to benchmarking and evaluating software products based on clearly defined criteria (Rafi et al., 2025). 

Despite these potential advantages, adopting Generative AI-based solutions in SE, especially those based on large language models (LLMs), has proven complex and challenging. Among them, issues related to ethics, security, privacy, dependence on external vendors, and the potential biases inherent to the AI models stand out (Russo, 2024; Terragni et al., 2025). Moreover, the effective adoption of these solutions depends on development teams’ ability to integrate these technologies into their workflows (Russo, 2024). Beyond these cross-cutting challenges, prior research has also reported solution-specific technical limitations affecting real-world adoption, including issues related to robustness across heterogeneous software projects, high rate of false positives, limited explainability of model outputs, dependence on public data quality, and the difficulties in producing developer-actionable results in practical environments (Garousi et al., 2025; Siavvas et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025). 

These systemic issues are underscored by recent empirical observations regarding the friction of adopting these technologies in real-world settings. For instance, Travassos et al. (2025) highlights significant integration barriers, a heavy reliance on ad-hoc prompting, and the burden of extensive manual validation to ensure quality in critical domains. In light of this scenario, mapping and analyzing the evidence-based Generative AI solutions available for use, while observing their limitations, challenges, and gaps, becomes a relevant task to investigate whether such scarcity of robust tools is a systemic characteristic of the field. For this study, we operationalize the key concepts of the research question as follows: 

- **Availability:** Public accessibility of the solution artifacts, such as source code, binaries, or access via internet-based platforms, combined with sufficient documentation to allow independent exploration, replication, and evaluation by third parties.

## **2 Related work** 

Several studies have investigated the role of Generative AI in Software Engineering (SE), aiming to understand how researchers and practitioners evaluate and apply this technology. To effectively position our contribution, we analyze the most relevant prior investigations, highlighting their scope, key findings, and limitations in the context of industrial readiness. Table 1 summarizes this analysis. 

Regarding general overviews, Hou et al. (2024) conducted a comprehensive Systematic Literature Review (SLR) covering 395 articles to categorize the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) across 85 SE tasks. While this work provides a broad academic landscape, its extensive scope inevitably dilutes the depth of analysis regarding software quality, focusing on research trends rather than operational maturity. Similarly, Zhang et al. (2024) unified perspectives from NLP and SE, offering a critical technical analysis of model architectures. Their work highlights important issues, such as the manipulation of benchmarks, but remains a theoretical synthesis that does not address the practical availability of solutions for production environments. From a sociotechnical perspective, Bazzan et al. (2024) performed a Multivocal Literature Review (MLR) to explore risks like hallucinations and bias. While essential for understanding ethical impacts, this study discusses risks as general phenomena and does not technically evaluate how specific existing solutions mitigate these risks in industrial workflows. 

Other studies have narrowed their scope to specific sub-domains. Schmid et al. (2025) focused on software architecture, identifying trends towards decoder-only models but restricting the analysis to architectural tasks and academic experimentation. In the security domain, Zhou et al. (2025) provided a specialized SLR on vulnerability detection and repair. They identified critical algorithmic limitations, such as the lack of high-quality datasets and input granularity. While definitive for security, their work centers on model performance metrics rather than the broader operational readiness of quality assurance solutions. Finally, addressing the state of practice in testing, Garousi et al. (2025) conducted a Systematic Tool Review of 56 commercial AI-powered tools. Their empirical study provides valuable insights into features like self-healing; however, the scope is strictly confined to proprietary testing products, limiting the analysis of the underlying Generative AI models. 

As Table 1 illustrates, the existing body of knowledge has predominantly adopted either a high-level theoretical perspective or a narrow domain-specific focus. A critical gap emerges from this analysis: the lack of a consolidated assessment of the industrial readiness of Generative AI specifically for Software Quality activities. Previous efforts have largely mapped _what is theoretically possible_ or _what commercial testing tools claim to do_ . This study addresses this gap by employing a multivocal data collection protocol to capture both academic prototypes and available tools, evaluating their maturity against the immediate needs of practitioners, such as infrastructure requirements and support mechanisms, thereby bridging the disconnect between theoretical research and industrial adoption.

## **3 Methodology** 

This study aligns with recent investigations in software quality that use systematic review protocols as a rigorous diagnostic tool to establish the state of the art before proposing technical advancements or guidelines (Ahmad and Kuo, 2025). However, given our specific

objective of assessing _industrial readiness_ rather than relying solely on academic theory, we adapted this approach to a Rapid Multivocal Literature Review (RMLR) protocol. 

Rapid Literature Reviews (RLRs) are an agile form of knowledge synthesis, optimized to deliver timely evidence for emerging fields. While traditional Systematic Literature Reviews (SLRs) emphasize exhaustive coverage, aiming to “ _identify, evaluate and interpret all available research"_ (Kitchenham, 2004), RLRs streamline the process to capture key trends and immediate applicability (Cartaxo et al., 2018; Hamel et al., 2021; Pizard et al., 2024). Given the rapid growth of Generative AI, a traditional SLR protocol risks obsolescence before data extraction is even complete. Thus, an RLR protocol represents a methodologically sound choice to establish a rigorous baseline of the state of practice. Although this approach may not cover all the steps of a full SLR, recent empirical assessments suggest that RLRs enable the identification of findings in Software Engineering (SE) comparable to those obtained through comprehensive SLRs, while providing more timely, actionable evidence for decision-making and connecting scientific evidence with practitioner needs (Pena et al., 2024; Pizard et al., 2024). 

An RMLR extends the RLR framework by incorporating grey literature (GL) into its search process, including materials not published in scientific or peer-reviewed venues, and is often grounded in the practical experience and technical insights of professionals. The use of grey literature in SE is strongly associated with evidence-based methods and literature reviews in the field, typically encompassing code repositories, white papers, and technical documentation, which are commonly identified through Google Search (Garousi et al., 2020, 2016). As noted by these authors, while GL is vital for capturing practical insights in emerging fields with strong industry involvement, it introduces risks regarding quality and source bias. To address these challenges and ensure credibility, we employed a quality appraisal procedure that filtered sources based on: 

1. _Authority and Credibility_ : Prioritizing sources from verifiable organizations or recognized practitioners over anonymous or unverified content. 

2. _Technical Evidence_ : Selecting sources that provide concrete use cases, tool categorizations, or architectural details, rather than purely opinion-based or high-level marketing descriptions. 

This GL appraisal process ensured that the final selection included only reliable, evidence-based solutions, sourced from both peer-reviewed publications and grey literature, explicitly excluding subjective or purely promotional material. To systematize the analysis of these diverse sources, this assessment implemented a structured protocol based on the Rapid Multivocal Literature Review methodology. By adhering to the meta-protocol defined by Rocha et al. (2025), we established a rigorous foundation enabled not just the identification but the critical assessment of Generative AI-based solutions available for industrial application. To operationalize this objective, the main research question was decomposed into ten specific subquestions, guiding the systematic extraction and technical characterization of the collected evidence: 

- **RQ1a** . Where can these solutions be found? 

- **RQ1b** . What Generative AI models are used by the solutions? 

- **RQ1c** . How can these solutions be used? 

- **RQ1d** . What technical and operational constraints should be considered when adopting these solutions?

## **3.3.1 Screening of scientific literature** 

The 522 studies retrieved from Scopus underwent a three-stage screening process executed by two primary reviewers, with a third reviewer acting as a mediator. To ensure consistency and transparency, the reviewers applied a standardized rating scale strictly mapped to the inclusion (Table 3) and exclusion (Table 4) criteria. This systematic approach prioritized evidence retention over immediate exclusion. Specifically: 

- 5 Stars (Accept): Assigned when the study appeared to satisfy all inclusion criteria (IC1–IC6), demonstrating clear relevance to Generative AI in software quality. 

- 3 Stars (Doubt): Assigned when relevance could not be determined due to insufficient detail (e.g., vague title regarding IC4, ensuring that ambiguous cases were not discarded prematurely). 

- 1 Star (Not accept): Assigned when the study clearly violated an inclusion criterion or met an exclusion criterion (e.g., EC5 - secondary study, or failing IC2 by using discriminative models). 

Based on this classification scheme, the screening process was executed in three sequential stages: 

1. **Stage 1 (Title Screening)** : First, the metadata of all 522 publications were imported into two separate JabRef[3] libraries, allowing two reviewers to assess them independently. Each reviewer classified the titles using a three-point scale: five stars (accept), three stars (doubt), and one star (reject). The inter-rater agreement for this stage yielded a Linear Weighted Kappa of 0.45 (Moderate Agreement). Given this level of agreement, which is consistent with the ambiguity of titles in emerging fields, we applied an inclusive consensus rule to prioritize recall: studies with a combined score of 6, 8, or 10 stars proceeded to the next stage. ## **3.4 Data coding and synthesis** 

Data extraction was performed using a structured schema derived directly from the research questions (RQ1a-RQ1j). Following extraction, the analysis was conducted through an iterative process of _thematic coding_ (Yang, 2025). This approach allowed themes, patterns, and categorizations to emerge directly from a deep reading and interpretation of the publications. Due to the heterogeneity of the data, we used this analysis to identify recurring patterns of readiness across the selected studies, highlighting representative examples that illustrate broader industrial trends. By transforming the raw descriptive content into structured analytical insights, we sought to gain a deeper understanding of the evidence contained in the collected material (Cruzes and Dyba, 2011). 

We applied this method to create a structured overview of the mapped Generative AIbased solutions. This framework was guided by the dimensions outlined in our research subquestions. A key step was classifying the _primary objective_ of each solution, as presented in Tables 8 and 9. To this end, we analyzed the descriptive excerpts from each source. Since Generative AI tools are often multi-purpose, we established a _prioritization rule_ : functionalities directly related to software quality were prioritized over generic capabilities. For example, if a solution offered both generic code generation and specific test case generation, it was coded under the latter to align with the scope of this work.

- Security: Solutions focused on vulnerability detection, penetration testing, or secure code enforcement. 

- Reliability: Solutions aiming to detect defects, bugs, or anomalies to ensure operational stability. 

- Maintainability: Solutions that facilitate modification and testing, including automated test generation and instrumentation.

## Fundamental limitations 

- _**Data Leakage and Limited Generalizability:**_ A primary limitation is the reliance on public benchmark datasets, which are susceptible to _data leakage_ . According to Happe and Cito (2023) and Kang et al. (2023), popular benchmarks and security write-ups were likely included in the models’ pre-training sets, which leads to overestimation of actual performance and creates a false sense of industrial readiness. This issue is further highlighted in Xia et al. (2024), which shows that models often leverage knowledge acquired during training when evaluating well-known open-source systems. These dependencies explain why many solutions struggle with _cross-project generalization_ when applied to proprietary or unseen codebases, where the model cannot rely on memorized patterns. Li et al. (2023) explains that this difficulty arises because LLMs are often insensitive to nuances, where subtle differences between similar code sequences can lead to similar inferences regardless of whether the code is correct or erroneous. 

- _**High False Positive Rates and Limited Explainability:**_ A major technical barrier, particularly in tasks such as vulnerability detection, concerns the frequent generation of false positives, which undermines developer trust and increases validation overhead. Recent empirical evaluations (Ahmed et al., 2025; Sheng et al., 2024) consistently report that even state-of-the-art GenAI models exhibit limited predictive performance in realistic settings, with modest precision and F1-scores and a tendency to over-identify potential defects. This issue is compounded by limited statement-level localization and the generation of syntactically plausible yet logically inconsistent rationales, hindering developers’ ability to understand and act upon model outputs. Furthermore, agent-based solutions for end-to-end task resolution (Ahmed et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024) remain susceptible to error propagation and execution hallucinations, ultimately imposing substantial manual verification effort and limiting suitability for industrial-grade quality and security audits. 

- _**Contextual Isolation and Scale Limitations:**_ Current solutions are often restricted to analyzing small, localized code segments, failing to scale to the complexity of industrial systems. As identified by Kang et al. (2023), these tools struggle to track correlations between different parts of the code, especially when a defect depends on helper functions or configurations located in separate files. This isolation means the model is often “blind" to the broader system context, leading to syntactically correct results that are logically invalid for the specific project. Furthermore, as noted in Xia et al. (2024), many approaches depend on near-perfect technical documentation; in real-world legacy systems where such documentation is missing or outdated, the models struggle to understand the code’s intended behavior, resulting in solutions that fail to address systemwide quality issues.

Similarly, evaluations in Chen et al. (2024) and Ahmed et al. (2025) show that the effectiveness of these tools is often tied to specific high-end models, with performance degrading significantly when using different or smaller model versions. This instability poses a critical risk to the reproducibility of quality audits, as organizations lack control over the model’s lifecycle and the consistency of results over time. 

- _**Complex Implementation and Lack of Usable Formats:**_ The solutions are generally not provided in ready-to-use formats. As described in RQ1c, they are typically distributed as code repositories or complex architectures (e.g., RAG) that demand specialized technical expertise for setup and replication. This lack of "out-of-the-box" usability is evident in studies like Jimenez et al. (2024), where specialized containerization (Docker) and environment configurations are required, creating a significant challenge for adoption by industry practitioners who require seamless integration into existing pipelines. 

- _**Absence of Formal Support and Maintenance:**_ Industrial-grade quality assurance requires long-term reliability. However, our analysis (RQ1g and RQ1i) reveals that nearly all identified solutions lack formal support channels, active communities, or maintenance plans. Most tools remain static prototypes as of their publication date, posing a significant risk for organizations that require constant security updates and stable support for their internal auditing tools. 

The analysis reveals that the vast majority of the identified solutions remain in the early stages of technological maturity, primarily as prototypes developed and tested in academic or controlled settings. While most solutions fit this description, a few initiatives show progress toward practical adoption. For instance, the solution in Patel et al. (2024) underwent pilot testing and was validated across three companies. Another study (Ahmed et al., 2025) made its solution publicly available on the Hugging Face platform, allowing for initial dissemination[9] and third-party experimentation. Despite these promising exceptions, the overall landscape is characterized by low technological maturity, with a general lack of solid evidence for the feasibility, scalability, and robustness of these solutions in industrial production environments.

## **5.2 Situating the findings: An immature but promising field** 

Our findings, while focused on a specific niche of software quality, strongly corroborate and provide concrete evidence for the broader perception that the application of Generative AI in Software Engineering remains in its early, experimental stages. Specifically, our empirical results regarding the generalization gap and lack of explainability align with recent theoretical concerns raised by Zhou et al. (2025) and Siavvas et al. (2024) regarding the limitations of LLMs for critical tasks. Broadly state-of-the-art works such as Zhang et al. (2024) and Schmid et al. (2025) have noted the conceptual nature of many LLM applications in SE. Our study amplifies this by detailing this immaturity specifically within the quality domain: we provide tangible evidence of a landscape dominated by academic prototypes (RQ1f) that are validated almost exclusively on synthetic or benchmark datasets (RQ1e), reinforcing the significant gap between academic research and practical adoption. The scarcity of industrial validation identified in our study (RQ1j) is a critical issue that warrants deeper discussion. We posit several interconnected causes for this phenomenon: 

- **The “Last Mile” Problem in Academic Research:** A significant barrier to the industrial impact of academic research is what can be described as the “last mile problem". This concept, drawn from other fields, symbolizes the inefficiencies in the final stage of delivering a product or service to the end-user (Li et al., 2025). In our context, it represents the gap between a functional academic prototype and a robust, usable tool ready for industrial adoption. Our findings indicate that this gap manifests in two critical dimensions. On the _operational front_ , we identified a lack of logistical readiness, evidenced by the scarcity of documentation, user support, and easy-to-use installers. Simultaneously, on the _technical front_ , many solutions exhibit characteristics that can limit their actual practicality. As evidenced by Kang et al. (2024) and Li et al. (2023), performance on public benchmarks is often inflated by data leakage; consequently, these prototypes demonstrate poor cross-project prediction, failing to generalize when applied to proprietary source code that was not known during training. Furthermore, the prevailing black-box nature of these models prevents users from understanding why a specific fragment was flagged or how to remediate the identified issue (Sheng et al., 2024). Consequently, bridging the “last mile” requires a holistic effort, improving engineering usability while simultaneously solving the algorithmic hurdles of generalizability and explainability. 

- **The “Strategic Adoption Dilemma” for Industry:** Companies face a significant dilemma when deciding how to adopt and validate these solutions. On the one hand, using powerful, _Closed Models_ (RQ1b) raises concerns about data privacy and security, as well as unpredictable costs. This approach introduces a substantial risk of _vendor lockin_ , a well-documented challenge in cloud computing where organizations become dependent on a single provider’s proprietary technology, making future migration difficult and costly (Opara-Martins et al., 2016). This risk is particularly acute with LLM APIs, which often lack the predictable update cycles and formal service-level agreements of traditional enterprise software. On the other hand, using _Open Models_ requires substantial upfront investment in specialized hardware (RQ1d). Our finding that only NVIDIA GPUs were mentioned directly reflects this challenge; the maturity of NVIDIA’s CUDA software platform has created a powerful ecosystem lock-in, making alternatives from 

```

```

Software Quality Journal           (2026) 34:24 

other vendors practically unviable for most researchers seeking stability and performance (Cusumano, 2023; Manganelli, 2025). Compounding this dilemma, both approaches carry the inherent risk of relying on models trained on vast, uncurated datasets that may introduce biases or security flaws. This complex web of trade-offs significantly slows down large-scale industrial experimentation and validation. 

- **Scarcity of Realistic Public Data:** A fundamental challenge in empirical software engineering is that high-quality industrial data, such as comprehensive defect reports or real-world security vulnerabilities, is often proprietary and highly sensitive. This scarcity forces many researchers to rely on publicly available benchmarks (as shown in Table 12) or to use synthetic data, which are artificially generated datasets designed to emulate original data without revealing sensitive observations (Wang et al., 2024). While these approaches are helpful for controlled experimentation, they may not accurately reflect the complexity and scale of industrial software systems. This creates a problematic feedback loop in which academic solutions are not trained on or validated with industrial-grade data, further widening the gap between research and practice. 

Furthermore, our analysis of the grey literature revealed a strong “academic echo," with a high concentration of preprints from repositories like arXiv[12] . The limited presence of industrial white papers, technical blogs, or open-source projects from industry actors reinforces the perception that widespread, practical adoption is still in its nascent stages, with much of the current knowledge being generated and disseminated by academic actors. 

## **6 Threats to validity** 

In this section, we discuss the potential threats to the validity of this study that may compromise the reliability and applicability of the results obtained. To ensure the robustness of the findings, it is essential to identify, discuss, and, whenever possible, minimize these impacts (Verdecchia et al., 2023). Furthermore, ensuring the validity of the results is crucial to guarantee their applicability to the target population (Travassos et al., 2002; Wohlin et al., 2000). In this study, we analyzed four main categories of threats that could contribute to future replication attempts of the RMLR: _internal validity, external validity, construct validity,_ and _conclusion validity_ (Shull et al., 2008). We present the strategies adopted to minimize their impacts and highlighted possible limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. 

## **6.1 Internal validity** 

- **Threats** : The "rapid" nature of the RMLR implied that no in-depth assessment of the methodological quality of the included studies was conducted. However, it is a common practice in the execution of RRs, which aim to deliver results in a shorter timeframe, even if this implies simplifying traditional steps. As a consequence, technologies developed and evaluated with lower methodological rigor may have been included and treated with the same weight as those with more robust methodologies. Furthermore,

- **Mitigations** : To mitigate these effects, we acknowledge these limitations as inherent to the RMLR approach. Our primary mitigation strategy was to design the research questions themselves to act as a proxy for quality assessment. Specifically, subquestions regarding maturity (RQ1f), support (RQ1g), and industrial collaboration (RQ1j) allowed us to indirectly assess the practical relevance and robustness of each solution’s evidence. For the grey literature, we adopted a source diversification strategy, consulting multiple channels (Google, GitHub, and PapersWithCode) to expand coverage and minimize platform-specific biases. 

## **6.2 External validity** 

- **Threats** : Threats to external validity concern the generalizability of our findings. The scope of our search was deliberately limited, which introduces four main considerations. First, the search in the scientific literature was restricted to the Scopus database. Second, the grey literature analysis considered only the first 100 results from each queried platform, potentially excluding relevant sources ranked lower by the search engine. Third, the study included only articles published in English after December 2021, which excludes solutions published in other languages or earlier periods. Finally, a temporal threat exists due to the rapid evolution of Generative AI, suggesting that new tools or versions may have emerged between our data collection (April-May 2025) and publication. 

- **Mitigations** : These limitations were deliberate scoping decisions, necessary to conduct a “rapid" assessment focused on the most recent and accessible solutions. This approach was justified in the Methodology section, where we emphasized that the objective was not to be exhaustive but to provide a structured overview of current practice. Regarding the temporal threat, we argue that the critical findings of this assessment, specifically the "Last Mile Problem" and the lack of industrial readiness, are systemic characteristics of the field rather than transient features of specific tools. Therefore, the structural diagnostic presented here remains a valid and necessary baseline for understanding the persistent gap between academic prototypes and industrial needs. 

## **6.3 Construct Validity** 

- **Threats** : The main threat in this category is related to the operationalization of the search strings. The concept of "software quality" is broad and multifaceted, making it challenging to ensure that all its dimensions are considered when formulating queries. Furthermore, other terms used, such as those related to Generative AI technologies, the types of expected solutions, or the application context, may also be ambiguous or inconsistently used across sources, especially in the grey literature. Additionally, the need to shorten the search string for Google, imposed by the search engine’s technical limitations, may have compromised the consistency and scope of data collection across

## **6.4 Conclusion validity** 

- **Threats** : Researcher bias in the selection and classification of studies is an inherent threat to any review, especially when it involves subjective judgments. The interpretation of inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as the categorization of findings, may vary among team members, potentially affecting the consistency of the results. 

- **Mitigations** : To mitigate this threat, we implemented a rigorous, multi-stage review protocol throughout the screening process. For the Scopus literature, each stage (title, abstract, and full text) involved independent evaluations by at least 2 authors. Disagreements were systematically resolved either through a predefined consensus rule, a thirdparty mediator, or a final consensus meeting, as detailed in Section 3.3. For the grey literature, the sources were initially distributed among three authors for independent readings. This was followed by a cross-review stage, in which a second author reassessed each source, ensuring that each item underwent a dual review before discrepancies were discussed in a consensus meeting. This structured, multi-reviewer approach, supported by tools like JabRef for managing scientific literature, was designed to ensure greater consistency and reliability in the final selection of publications, thereby strengthening the validity of our conclusions. 

## **7 Conclusion** 

This study aimed to assess the industrial readiness and availability of Generative AI-based solutions currently available to support quality-related activities in Software Engineering. Our findings reveal a field in its early stages, characterized by a vibrant academic interest that has yet to translate into a mature ecosystem of industry-ready tools. The landscape is marked by a significant gap between the state of the art in academic research and the state of practice in industry, primarily due to a lack of industrial validation, high adoption barriers, and structural technical limitations regarding model precision and generalizability.

## **7.1 Implications of this work** 

The results of this study carry significant implications for both researchers and practitioners. _For researchers,_ our findings highlight a critical need to bridge the “last mile" gap between algorithmic prototypes and usable solutions. Future work should prioritize industrial collaborations, address the reliability gap through improved explainability and context-awareness, and validate on real-world data rather than relying solely on synthetic benchmarks, and create well-documented, reproducible artifacts. _For practitioners,_ this work serves as a pragmatic guide to the current landscape, underscoring that Generative AI should be adopted as an assistive technology that augments human expertise, not as a fully autonomous replacement. Practitioners must carefully consider the “strategic adoption dilemma," balancing the costs and risks of proprietary models against the infrastructure and expertise demands of open-source alternatives. 

## **7.2 Limitations of this study** 

While we employed a rigorous methodology, this study has inherent limitations that should be considered. First, our assessment relies on the reported evidence, documentation, and repository data of the identified solutions; we did not perform a hands-on execution or benchmark testing of the tools themselves. Second, by focusing on publicly documented solutions, our search may have missed proprietary, in-house tools used silently within specific companies. Additionally, while the use of Scopus as the primary academic database provided a broad baseline, it is possible that some specific tool-related papers indexed in other databases were not captured, a risk we mitigated by triangulating with extensive grey literature searches. Finally, regarding the rapid evolution of Generative AI, we acknowledge that individual tool versions may have changed since our data collection. However, as discussed in Section 5, we contend that the identified structural challenges, such as the “Last Mile Problem", are systemic and persist independently of short-term model updates. 

## **7.3 A roadmap for future research** 

Based on the gaps and opportunities identified, we propose a clear roadmap to advance the field towards higher quality standards: 

1. **Industrial Case Studies and Benchmarks:** There is an urgent need for in-depth case studies evaluating academic prototypes in real-world industrial settings. Furthermore, although challenging due to confidentiality constraints and the limited public availability of proprietary systems, the community would greatly benefit from the development of standardized benchmarks grounded in realistic industrial scenarios. Possible paths forward include anonymized datasets, curated subsets of proprietary systems under controlled collaboration agreements, or systematically constructed benchmarks derived from representative open-source repositories (e.g., SWE-bench Jimenez et al., 2024). Such initiatives would enhance the ecological validity, fairness, and reproducibility of evaluations while strengthening the bridge between academic research and industrial practice.

2. **Quality Guidelines and Standardization:** Future research must move beyond adhoc experimentation to establish quality models for GenAI-based SE solutions. This includes defining specific standards for robustness, explainability, and integration readiness. Furthermore, there is a critical need for systematic criteria to guide model selection, helping practitioners evaluate the trade-offs between closed and open architectures based on specific industrial constraints. 

3. **Expanding the Scope Across the SDLC and ISO 25010:** As our mapping revealed a heavy concentration of solutions in the _Coding_ and _Testing_ phases, future research must expand to earlier stages of the SDLC. There is a significant opportunity to explore GenAI solutions in _Requirements_ engineering and _Design_ architecture. Furthermore, the field must move beyond isolated code-level fixes (primarily targeting Security and Reliability) to address the full spectrum of systemic quality attributes defined by the ISO/IEC 25010 standard, such as usability, performance efficiency, and portability. 

4. **Addressing the “Last Mile” Problem:** Research is needed on the software engineering challenges of transforming GenAI prototypes into robust, industry-ready tools. This requires a dual approach: (i) addressing operational usability through IDE integration, human-computer interaction for AI assistants, and seamless CI/CD deployment; and (ii) solving fundamental reliability hurdles, specifically by enhancing cross-project generalization and providing transparent, actionable rationales for AI-generated outputs. 

In conclusion, closer, more structured collaboration between academia and industry will be essential to realize the potential of Generative AI and deliver reliable, scalable, and effective solutions that can be integrated into the daily operations of software quality assurance.
