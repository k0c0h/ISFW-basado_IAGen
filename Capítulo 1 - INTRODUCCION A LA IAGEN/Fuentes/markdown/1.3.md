# **Generative AI as an infrastructure copilot: automating Infrastructure-As-Code across the DevSecOps lifecycle** 

**Matteo Esposito[2] · Mikel Robredo[2] · Alexander Bakhtin[2] · Davide Taibi[1,2] · Valentina Lenarduzzi[1,2]** 

Received: 12 October 2025 / Accepted: 6 February 2026 / Published online: 27 February 2026 © The Author(s) 2026 

## **Abstract** 

Practitioners and researchers continuously focus on developing automation strategies to cope with the exponentially demanding need for the timely deployment of software projects in tight release schedules. Such automation techniques include Infrastructure-as-Code (IaC) and the DevOps and DevSecOps cycles. Recent studies investigated generative AI (GenAI) for generating infrastructure as code scripts. However, no studies have focused on using GenAI to generate IaC scripts based on DevSecOps stage artifacts. Different IaC tools serve varied purposes, requiring specific infrastructure setups for different project stages. We envision GenAI models leveraging artifacts from each DevSecOps stage to create and refine IaC scripts. We trust our approach to have an impact on practitioners to leverage it as an automatic copilot for infrastructure design and deployment, and for researchers to build on our vision and future empirical validation. 

**Keywords** GenAI · IaC · DevOps · DevSecOps · Security · Agentic AI

## **1 Introduction** 

IaC allows practitioners to manage their technology and infrastructural stacks through scripts, making them efficient, scalable, and standardized with fewer threats of human error (Morris 2020). However, human effort is required and spent on creating and working with IaC scripts. Recent experiments with Generative AI (GenAI) were mildly successful in creating, interacting with, and automating IaC (Chanus and Aubertin 2023). Recently, Esposito et al. (2025) proposed a MAPE-K-based framework that leverages agentic AI for autonomous anomaly detection and remediation in microservice environments. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has proposed an end-to-end, lifecycle-aware approach that synthesizes and iteratively refines Infrastructure as Code by leveraging artifacts produced across DevSecOps (DSO) stages (U.S. Department of Defense, Office of the Chief Information Officer 2021). Moreover, different IaC tools can serve various purposes and coexist in the same project (Rahman et al. 2019). We envision GenAI analyzing each DSO stage artifact and preparing the most fitting infrastructure, e.g., for testing, deployment, or monitoring. 

On the one hand, our vision allows practitioners to use a copilot to enable infrastructure design and deployment. More specifically, they can adapt and standardize the artifacts of each stage to better represent the needs of the infrastructure. On the other hand, researchers can build on our results and future empirical validation to improve the state of the art by providing new specialized GenAI tools or artifacts to enhance the quality of IaC-deployed infrastructure. 

**Paper Structure** . In Section 2, we provide theoretical background for the study and our framework, and in Section 3, we present its design. Section 4 presents the roadmap for implementing and empirically validating the proposed framework. In Section 5, we discuss limitations and the ethical implications. In Section 6, we conclude our vision. 

## **2 Background and motivation** 

The concept of IaC was initiated by C. Fowler, who coined the concept of immutable infrastructure and disposable components, which he considered as the replacement for the traditional mutable infrastructure practice with unmodified servers and components after deployment (Fowler 2013). Such “modern” approach can benefit practitioners by providing simplified operations, continuous deployment, reduced errors, and minimal downtime with cloud reboots, which align closely with modern cloud architecture with the principles of DevOps and CI/CD practices. Many studies have proposed adopting IaC approaches, which are used in various sub-domains of software engineering. For example, IaC can be used to construct benchmark tools to evaluate the performance of native cloud applications (Scheuner et al. 2015). Other studies also proposed that IaC can be adopted to implement DevOps in terms of configuration management, code versioning, and revision management. Artac et al. (2017). Similarly, previous research contributed to automated provisioning, continuous deployment, and microservice implementation (Parnin et al. 2017). IaC is also

For DevOps, the adoption of IaC can provide many critical benefits to practitioners, but also presents a variety of challenges (Guerriero et al. 2019; Rahman 2018). For example, consistency and standardization across various environments are some benefits that can help reduce potential errors in the configuration process (Guerriero et al. 2019). When IaC is adopted to ensure this consistency, automation of infrastructure provisioning will greatly improve deployment efficiency, avoiding unnecessary manual intervention (Esposito et al. 2026b). Furthermore, IaC can also ease the effort for infrastructure scalability with reusable scripts and adaptability to additional required resources. Moreover, IaC scripts can be checked into version control systems such as git. From the management perspective, effective collaboration between distributed teams contributes to cost savings. 

On the other hand, the code quality of the scripts is critical for each stage of the DSO process supported by IaC. With generative AI, the scripts can be generated automatically, saving developer effort and guaranteeing quality given the proper guidelines. Developers thus can put more effort into designing software business logic, which has a greater influence on the robustness of the architecture and the perceived software quality of end users. 

## **3 Design** 

In this section, we present our **vision** on the role of GenAI in automating the generation of IaC scripts within the DSO cycle. It is worth noting that infrastructures can serve different purposes based on the developer’s needs during development, testing, and deployment (Rahman et al. 2019). This section takes advantage of the nine stages of DSO (U.S. Department of Defense, Office of the Chief Information Officer 2021; Inc. 2022) and identifies the relevant artifacts for each stage, the security concerns, and the role of GenAI in creating IaC scripts. In the upcoming empirical validation, the ultimate goal is to provide a set of GenAI models and guidelines to automate, as much as possible, the creation of IaC starting from the currently available artifacts, whether it is building a testing environment or a production one. Taking as baseline the DSO model (Inc. 2022), Fig. 1 displays the diagram showing the introduction of GenAI’s role into the DSO workflow, as well as a visualized list of identified input artifacts and envisioned IaC automation outcomes. Within the current section, we will expand our vision following each of the stages reported in the DSO model.

## **3.2 Develop** 

**Artifacts** The development (or code) stage within a DSO model involves the security testing of the existing source code. Therefore, potential artifacts in this stage could range from the project’s source code to the configuration files, as well as the security reports from already used static security analysis tools (SSAT). 

**Security** In the coding stage, practitioners could use SSAT that are limited to predefined rules to monitor potential bugs, security vulnerabilities, common coding errors such as SQL injection, cross-site scripting, memory leaks, null pointer dereferences (Esposito et al. 2024a), as well as architectural smells (Esposito et al. 2025; Fontana et al. 2017). 

**GenAI role** Within this stage, the GenAI model shall exploit the information from the identified artifacts and extract key contextual information, such as dependencies, required resources, and configurations, to create and modify IaC scripts. This would ensure that any necessary change to the infrastructure, needed to support new features or enable software updates, could be quickly deployed and automatically managed. Moreover, the GenAI model could leverage the output of the employed SSATs to avoid defining insecure or smell-prone IaC, thus acting as an additional code reviewer. 

## **3.3 Build** 

**Artifacts** The building stage could provide artifacts such as the developed build scripts based on the existing source code, as well as derived outputs from the build stage, such as dependency graphs or the Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) of a project, for instance. 

**Security** The artifacts considered in this stage are mainly the build output from the developed code in the previous workflow stage. These can include output reports from already existing analysis tools for the build stage of a project. Thus, we could leverage the output from SSATs specialized in compiled code or bytecode (Hutcheson et al. 2024) to catch any bug or vulnerabilities detectable after each build time. 

**GenAI role** Recent industry insights (Guerriero et al. 2019) have shown that heterogeneity, such as _polyglotism_ is one of the crucial challenges in IaC automation. For instance, polyglotism involves using multiple programming languages within a single environment and is essential for managing build scripts and dependencies in diverse technical projects. Furthermore, _portability_ stands also as a crucial aspect as it ensures that the software product can be easily transferred and executed across different systems and environments without modifications. ## **3.4 Test** 

**Artifacts** The test phase is initiated once a build artifact has been successfully built and delivered to staging or testing environments. Within this process, the artifacts providing project context could be the defined test case suite, the already reported test results, as well as the obtained testing evaluation metrics. 

**Security** Within the testing stage, practitioners usually design security test cases to add them to the test case suite. These test cases analyze the system response when it receives carefully crafted unexpected messages or data concerning access, authentication, and accounting (that is, the AAA in security) (Esposito and Falessi 2024; Sönmez and Kiliç 2021). Practitioners should gather the outcome from the employed security test cases to enable dynamic application security based on the generated reports. 

**GenAI role** In the testing stage, practitioners should provide GenAI models with the defined test cases, expected results, report cases, test evaluation metrics, and dynamic application security testing output. The model shall refine the IaC scripts with the required configurations for the test and deployment environments based on the changes observed in the provided artifacts. The application of the updated IaC script shall be automated, such that the outcome from the incoming new builds returns improved security testing reports. 

## **3.5 Release** 

**Artifacts** Once the testing stage is undergone, the release stage involves protecting the runtime environment architecture by reviewing the environment configuration. This stage entails a set of diverse components that can serve the GenAI model as context for the required IaC script automation process. Such components could involve software release notes, deployment scripts, version control tags, release checklists, and test reports. 

**Security** During the release stage, practitioners should manage any identified vulnerabilities and implement remediation efforts. In addition, a second threat modeling phase should be performed. Back during the planning stage, the threat model was

## **3.6 Deploy** 

**Artifacts** In the deployment phase, and only if the earlier process succeeded, the deployment of the software product happens. Within this context, practitioners can make use of created deployment manifests, container images, as well as Helm charts, environment configuration, and service discovery information generated during the deployment phase. 

**Security** In the deployment stage, tools like Terraform[4] , Ansible[5] , Puppet[6] , or Chef[7] could test the project for known security flows, and consequently execute IaC scripts. Such checks are based on policies or against benchmarks such as the CIS benchmarks (CIS 2018). Additionally, widely used container image scanning tools such as Clair[8] , Trivy[9] , and Aqua[10] can provide insightful content for the GenAI model. 

**GenAI role** In this stage, the GenAI model should inspect the container images to understand how to configure and prepare the architecture to run them. Moreover, the model could use Helm charts to improve the already existing infrastructure and accommodate more efficient Kubernetes deployment and environment dependencies. Finally, the GenAI model shall leverage the output from the employed security checks to avoid deploying insecure infrastructure, thus refining and fixing IaC scripts for subsequent deployment stages.

## **3.7 Operate, monitor and respond** 

**Artifacts** We grouped the Operation, Monitor, and Respond phases given the already significant importance, constant control over these stages has within the DSO workflow. These stages involve the constant monitoring, anomaly detection, as well as performance statistics obtained from the software deployment. From the processes, practitioners could leverage monitoring data, process logs, incident reports, as well as configuration management data, deployment performance metrics, anomaly alerts, environment usage statistics, and performance reports. 

**Security** These stages altogether involve continuous log aggregation and analysis of the running system, security monitoring, real-time threat detection, endpoint security management, user activities, and secret management, as well as software reaction to these observations. 

**GenAI role** The GenAI model shall continuously refine the infrastructure in realtime as the monitoring data becomes available, improving the system performance and resilience to cyber threats. Essentially, the model should act in a proactive style since users could automatically provide anomaly alerts, environment usage statistics, and performance reports in advance. The GenAI model could analyze the provided input with additional security data, such as detected security anomaly logs, to identify potential problems, learn, and sketch updated IaC script solutions. Thus, users could exploit continuous monitoring to dynamically adjust the infrastructure with the resource needs gathered from the real-time data and remediate possible threats by issuing temporary IaC commands and scripts through the GenAI model. 

## **4 Roadmap** 

This section presents the future work for implementing and empirically validating our proposed vision for GenAI-enabled DevSecOps (see Section 3). Figure 2 presents the envisioned DSO workflow diagram, which visually shows the introduction of the GenAI role component into the architecture. The implementation must leverage real-world projects adopting IaC tools, while ensuring compliance with established **quality, security, and best-practice standards** . Thus, the implementation should be translated into a **proof of concept that integrates GenAI models into the DevSecOps workflow** through stage-specific hooks and triggers, enabling the automated generation and continuous refinement of IaC scripts based on available artifacts. Similarly, the empirical validation should be conducted by **systematically comparing GenAI-generated IaC scripts with those produced by human experts and state-of-the-art automation tools across DevSecOps stages** , assessing aspects such as correctness, security compliance, adaptability to change, and overall effectiveness. Thus, the evaluation will aim to determine whether GenAI can reliably act as an infrastructure copilot, as well as to identify current limitations, open challenges, and directions for future research.

## **5 Limitations and ethical implications** 

The proposed vision and its design present several limitations and ethical considerations. GenAI models may undergo difficulties with **complex or security-critical infrastructure requirements** (Zhang et al. 2025), as well as limitations in training **data quality** (Siddiq et al. 2024), **scalability** , and **AI explainability** (Esposito et al. 2024b; Amershi et al. 2019), which might lead to inaccurate, insecure, or biased IaC scripts. These risks can potentially occur in large-scale and distributed DevSecOps environments, where scalability, continuous adaptation, and security validation remain challenging (Esposito and Palagiano 2024). To mitigate these issues, in our vision, we emphasize the importance of **data governance** , **modular and incremental integration** , **security validation profiles** (Asare et al. 2023), **explainability mechanisms** (Esteves et al. 2020), and **a human-in-the-loop** (Esposito et al. 2025a) for critical infrastructure decisions. From an ethical perspective, it is crucial to discuss ethical issues arising in all aspects of Software Engineering (Johnson and Menzies 2024). GenAI and agentic AI may introduce concerns related to hallucinations, privacy leakage, backdoor vulnerabilities, and the impact of automation on the software engineering workforce (De Souza et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025). Consistent with prior work (Esposito et al. 2024b), we stress the point that **GenAI should be** used as a **valuable assistant** , lifting the heavy work from experts, and **not as expert replacement** .

## **6 Conclusions** 

In this work, we presented our vision and how it can potentially provide a paradigm shift in infrastructure setup by integrating GenAI into a DSO cycle to automatically generate and maintain IaC scripts. We proposed a roadmap for implementation and the empirical validation of the proposed pipelines. Our empirical validation aims to compare GenAI-generated IaC scripts with state-of-the-art tools and human experts to understand whether they can be exploited as fundamental tools and or copilots or if they fail at the current stage, therefore highlighting possible weaknesses, literature gaps, and future research direction. Researchers can leverage our paper as a blueprint for paving the way to an extensive empirical validation in this area to support and investigate how specific artifacts can be effectively employed by the models to generate specific infrastructures according to the DSO cycle, thus introducing a consolidated version of artifacts eventually to improve their usability by models. 

Finally, we trust our approach to have an impact on practitioners who can leverage it as an automatic copilot for infrastructure design and deployment and for researchers who can build on our results and future empirical validation to improve further and expand the possibilities enabled by it.
